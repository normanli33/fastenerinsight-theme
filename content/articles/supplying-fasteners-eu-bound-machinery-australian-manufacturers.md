# Supplying Fasteners for EU-Bound Machinery: What Australian Manufacturers Need to Know

For Australian machine builders exporting to Europe, compliance starts well before the finished equipment leaves the factory. The fasteners used in an assembly can form part of the evidence needed to demonstrate material suitability, chemical compliance and product traceability.

For fastener suppliers such as United Fasteners, the practical role is to provide accurate product information and agreed supporting documents. Understanding which requirements apply helps customers request the right evidence early and avoid delays during production or export.

## Do the new EU Product Identifier rules affect Australian fastener imports?

Importing fasteners from the UK, Europe or China into Australia does not, by itself, bring those shipments within the EU's new Product Identifier requirements.

According to FedEx's current guidance, the PID requirement applies to distance-sale B2C shipments imported into the EU with an intrinsic value not exceeding €150, becoming mandatory from 1 November 2026. The identifiers cover the merchant's product code, the manufacturer or supplier's product code, and a standardised identifier where one exists. [FedEx's EU customs guidance](https://www.fedex.com/en-bg/support/clearance/customs-changes.html)

A typical commercial shipment of industrial machinery to an EU business therefore needs to be assessed separately from these low-value consumer shipment rules. Using fasteners in a machine does not automatically create a requirement to declare a separate PID for every bolt, nut or washer.

However, customers may still need detailed component information to support the finished machine's compliance.

## Who is responsible when a customer exports a machine?

Consider this supply chain:

**Overseas fastener manufacturer → Australian fastener supplier → Australian machine builder → EU customer**

The machine manufacturer is responsible for assessing the finished machinery and meeting the applicable manufacturer obligations. EU importers and other relevant parties also have duties under the legislation that applies to the product.

Fastener documentation can support that assessment, but it does not establish compliance for the complete machine.

Timing also matters. The European Commission states that machinery placed on the EU market before 20 January 2027 must comply with the current Machinery Directive. The new Machinery Regulation generally applies from that date. [European Commission machinery guidance](https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/sectors/mechanical-engineering/machinery_en)

## The fastener information that matters most

### Clear product identification

Customers should be able to connect the fasteners supplied with their drawings, purchasing records and assembly specifications.

Useful records include:

- Supplier SKU and manufacturer or supplier part number.
- Product description, dimensions and thread specification.
- Applicable product standard and edition where relevant.
- Material or property class.
- Surface finish and coating specification.
- GTIN, EAN or other standardised identifier, where available.

A DIN or ISO standard describes product requirements; it does not, on its own, uniquely identify a manufacturer's product.

### Accurate country-of-origin information

The country a fastener is purchased from may differ from its country of origin. A Chinese-made bolt supplied through a UK distributor does not become UK-origin simply because it is dispatched from the UK.

Origin should be supported by appropriate supplier evidence. The finished machine's origin must be assessed separately under the relevant rules; it cannot be determined simply by listing where its fasteners were made. [European Commission rules of origin](https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/customs/rules-origin-goods/non-preferential-rules-origin_en)

### Material and batch traceability

Where the application requires traceability, records should connect the supplied fasteners to the relevant production lot, test results and delivery.

Customers should agree the required traceability level before ordering, particularly for safety-critical assemblies. Retrospective certification may be unavailable if the stock was purchased or handled without the necessary records.

The required evidence should reflect the consequences of failure. A small retaining screw in a critical mechanism may warrant more scrutiny than a larger bolt in a non-critical cover.

## REACH and RoHS: request evidence relevant to the product

REACH information can be important when fasteners become components of machinery sold in the EU.

Under REACH Article 33, EU suppliers of articles containing a Candidate List substance above 0.1% by weight must communicate sufficient safe-use information, including at least the substance's name. The assessment can apply to individual component articles within a larger assembly. [ECHA supply-chain guidance](https://echa.europa.eu/regulations/reach/candidate-list-substances-in-articles/communication-in-the-supply-chain)

Australian suppliers can help customers meet these obligations by obtaining product-specific substance information from manufacturers. A useful declaration identifies the products covered, its date and the Candidate List version assessed.

RoHS concerns hazardous substances in electrical and electronic equipment. Whether it applies depends on the finished equipment's scope and any relevant exclusions or exemptions. Customers should confirm applicability before requesting supporting fastener declarations. [European Commission RoHS review](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2023-12/COM_2023_760_1_EN_ACT_part1_v4.pdf)

Coatings and surface treatments deserve attention alongside the base material. A general statement about steel or stainless steel may not adequately describe the supplied finish.

## Which certificates are needed?

There is no single certificate package suitable for every fastener and application.

Purchase specifications may call for a declaration of conformity, test report, inspection certificate or particular mechanical, chemical or coating test results. Where an EN 10204 document is required, the document type and its relationship to the supplied goods should be agreed before purchase.

Customers should specify:

- The required certificate or report type.
- The tests and acceptance criteria.
- The batch or lot traceability required.
- Any inspection or independent verification arrangements.

Requesting every certificate type for every product can add cost without providing useful evidence. The objective is to obtain documentation that supports the actual design and purchasing requirements.

## A practical fastener documentation checklist

For machinery intended for the EU, agree the following with your fastener supplier before placing the order:

- **Product identity:** SKU, part number, description, dimensions and applicable standard.
- **Technical specification:** material or property class, coating and performance requirements.
- **Origin evidence:** country of origin and supporting documentation where required.
- **Traceability:** lot, batch or heat references appropriate to the application.
- **Chemical information:** relevant REACH information and RoHS evidence where applicable.
- **Quality records:** agreed certificates, inspection reports and test results.
- **Delivery records:** labels and paperwork that preserve the link between the goods and their supporting evidence.

Requirements should be reviewed when the manufacturer, material, coating, production source or applicable regulation changes.

The most effective approach is to include compliance and documentation requirements in the original fastener specification.

For Australian manufacturers preparing machinery for the EU, early discussions with suppliers help establish what evidence is needed, what is available and which products require additional controls.
