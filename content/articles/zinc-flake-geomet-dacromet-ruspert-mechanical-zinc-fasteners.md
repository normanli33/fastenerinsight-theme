# Zinc Flake, GEOMET, Dacromet, RUSPERT and Mechanical Zinc: An Engineering Guide for Fasteners

A fastener coating is not just a colour or a corrosion barrier. It can affect the **mechanical reliability of the fastener, thread fit, installation torque, clamp load, galvanic behaviour and environmental compliance**.

This is particularly important for high-strength bolts and screws. A coating system that performs well on a property class 8.8 bolt may not be the best choice for a property class 12.9 socket screw. Likewise, a coating that survives 1,000 hours in a neutral salt-spray test may still perform poorly in a treated-timber, marine or dissimilar-metal application.

For procurement and engineering teams, the useful question is therefore not:

> Which coating has the highest salt-spray hours?

It is:

> Which coating system provides the required corrosion protection, installation behaviour and process safety for this particular fastener and joint?

This article compares four commonly encountered coating families: **zinc flake coatings, GEOMET/Dacromet, RUSPERT and mechanically deposited zinc**.

## 1. Zinc flake coatings: the non-electrolytic option

Zinc flake coatings are thin-film, non-electrolytically applied coating systems containing overlapping zinc flakes, often combined with aluminium flakes, in an inorganic or organic binder.

The coating is commonly applied by **dip-spin, rack dip-spin or spray**, followed by curing. The overlapping metallic flakes provide a barrier against moisture while the zinc also provides sacrificial protection to the underlying steel.

For fasteners, zinc flake systems are principally covered by **ISO 10683** and, in ASTM practice, **ASTM F3393**.

### Why zinc flake is important for high-strength fasteners

The key advantage is that the **coating deposition process itself does not generate hydrogen in the way electroplating can**. This is why ISO 10683 specifically identifies zinc flake systems as particularly suitable for high-strength fasteners of approximately 1,000 MPa tensile strength and above.

However, it is important not to describe zinc flake as giving an unconditional “zero hydrogen-embrittlement risk”. The coating process includes surface preparation, and **acid pickling can introduce hydrogen before the coating is applied**.

For highly hardened fasteners, pretreatment therefore matters just as much as the coating chemistry. Mechanical cleaning, alkaline cleaning or other approved low-hydrogen preparation routes are generally preferred where internal hydrogen embrittlement is a concern.

### Typical advantages

- Thin coating build, which helps maintain thread fit.
- Strong corrosion resistance relative to coating thickness.
- Good suitability for high-strength fasteners when the complete coating process is properly controlled.
- Friction can be engineered using integral lubricants or topcoats.
- Silver, black and other functional finishes are available depending on the coating system.

### Important limitation

Zinc flake is a **coating family**, not one universal performance level. Salt-spray resistance, friction coefficient, thickness and chemical resistance depend on the specific basecoat, topcoat, number of layers, substrate and application process.

For this reason, a purchase order should not simply state “zinc flake”. The required standard, corrosion performance, friction requirement and any topcoat should also be defined.

## 2. GEOMET and Dacromet: two generations of zinc flake technology

GEOMET and Dacromet are proprietary zinc-flake technologies developed by NOF Metal Coatings. They are often discussed together, but they should not be treated as interchangeable names.

### Dacromet: the legacy generation

Dacromet established zinc-flake coating technology in many automotive and industrial applications. Older drawings may still specify Dacromet by name or by a legacy OEM coating code.

The important procurement issue is that many historical Dacromet specifications were associated with **hexavalent-chromium chemistry**. Environmental regulations and OEM requirements have therefore driven a large-scale transition toward chromium-free alternatives.

Where an old drawing specifies Dacromet, the correct action is not to substitute another coating automatically. Confirm whether the drawing permits an alternative, then compare corrosion performance, friction characteristics, thickness and qualification requirements.

### GEOMET: the modern chromium-free generation

GEOMET is the modern water-based, chromium-free zinc-flake family developed as the successor to Dacromet.

Two commonly encountered products are:

**GEOMET 321** — a zinc/aluminium-flake basecoat used where corrosion resistance is the primary requirement. Friction can be tailored by adding an appropriate topcoat.

**GEOMET 500** — a self-lubricated zinc-flake basecoat designed to provide corrosion protection together with controlled installation friction.

NOF Metal Coatings currently lists GEOMET 500 with a nominal coating thickness of approximately **6–15 µm**, corrosion performance dependent on coating configuration, and a controlled friction range of approximately **0.12–0.18 when tested to ISO 16047**.

That friction control can be as important as corrosion resistance. Two bolts with the same dimensions and property class can generate very different clamp loads at the same installation torque if their coefficients of friction are different.

This is why engineered fastener specifications increasingly treat **friction as a measurable coating property**, not merely a by-product of lubrication.

## 3. RUSPERT: a multilayer coating system

RUSPERT is a proprietary multilayer corrosion-protection family developed by Nihon Ruspert. It is widely associated with construction screws, self-drilling screws and applications requiring a combination of corrosion protection, surface durability and colour options.

It is better to describe RUSPERT as a **multilayer system** rather than simply as “ceramic-coated zinc”. Current RUSPERT technologies include systems based on zinc-nickel alloy plating combined with conversion/primer and functional topcoat layers.

The outer coating can provide additional properties such as:

- corrosion resistance;
- controlled friction;
- reduced galling;
- colour identification;
- improved contact-corrosion behaviour; and
- resistance to mechanical damage during installation.

These characteristics make multilayer systems attractive for self-drilling and construction screws, where the coating must survive not only outdoor exposure but also the severe mechanical contact that occurs during driving.

### A hydrogen-embrittlement caution

Unlike zinc-flake deposition, RUSPERT systems may incorporate an **electroplated zinc or zinc-alloy substrate**, depending on the specific system. High-strength or case-hardened screws therefore require appropriate control of cleaning, plating and post-treatment processes.

Do not assume that every product sold as RUSPERT has the same hydrogen-embrittlement profile. Check the exact coating specification and the fastener hardness.

## 4. Mechanical zinc: zinc deposited without electroplating

Mechanically deposited zinc, commonly called **mechanical plating** or, at heavier coating classes, **mechanical galvanizing**, is covered by standards such as **ASTM B695** and **ISO 12683**.

Instead of using electrical current to plate zinc onto the steel, the parts are tumbled with zinc powder, impact media and process chemicals. Repeated mechanical impact causes zinc particles to bond to the steel surface.

The result is a sacrificial zinc coating applied without the electrochemical deposition step used in conventional zinc electroplating.

### Why engineers use it

Mechanical zinc is useful where a relatively substantial zinc coating is required but an electrolytic plating process is undesirable.

Advantages can include:

- reduced risk of hydrogen introduction from the deposition step;
- a range of selectable coating thickness classes;
- no hot-dip galvanizing bath temperature;
- good sacrificial zinc protection; and
- applicability to many hardened steel components when the complete process is appropriately controlled.

Again, pretreatment matters. A mechanical coating process does not automatically make every upstream cleaning step hydrogen-free. The complete finishing route should be reviewed for high-strength or embrittlement-sensitive fasteners.

## 5. Engineering comparison

| Characteristic | Zinc flake / GEOMET | Dacromet | RUSPERT | Mechanical zinc |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Basic technology** | Non-electrolytic zinc/aluminium flake coating | Legacy zinc-flake technology | Proprietary multilayer system | Mechanically deposited metallic zinc |
| **Main protection mechanism** | Barrier + sacrificial zinc protection | Barrier + sacrificial protection | Multilayer barrier + metallic sacrificial layer, depending on system | Sacrificial zinc protection |
| **Hydrogen generated by coating deposition** | No | No | Depends on specific substrate process; some systems use electroplating | No electroplating during zinc deposition |
| **Typical coating build** | Thin film; product-dependent | Thin film | System-dependent, multilayer | Thickness selected by coating class |
| **Friction control** | Excellent when specified with integral lubricant or topcoat | Depends on legacy system/topcoat | Can be engineered with functional topcoats | Usually requires separate consideration of lubrication/friction |
| **Thread-fit advantage** | Very good because coating is thin | Very good | Depends on system thickness | Generally better controlled than very thick hot-dip zinc, but thread allowance must still be checked |
| **Colour options** | Silver, black and others depending on topcoat | Typically silver-grey legacy finishes | Wide range depending on system | Usually metallic zinc appearance unless supplementary treatment is applied |
| **Common applications** | High-strength fasteners, automotive, transport, industrial equipment | Legacy automotive/industrial specifications | Construction screws, self-drilling screws, architectural and outdoor applications | Fasteners, washers, springs and hardware requiring sacrificial zinc without electroplating |

The table should be used as a selection guide, not as a substitute for the governing standard or coating supplier's qualified system.

## 6. Do not select a coating by salt-spray hours alone

Neutral salt spray testing, such as **ISO 9227** or **ASTM B117**, is useful for checking coating consistency and comparing systems under defined laboratory conditions. It is not a direct prediction of outdoor service life.

A coating rated for 1,000 hours of neutral salt spray is not automatically “twice as durable” in service as one rated for 500 hours.

Real fastener performance depends on factors such as:

- coating damage during installation;
- wet/dry cycling;
- temperature;
- chloride exposure;
- joint geometry and crevices;
- contact with aluminium, stainless steel or copper-containing materials;
- chemical exposure;
- coating thickness at thread roots and bearing surfaces; and
- maintenance or reassembly cycles.

For fasteners, salt-spray performance should therefore be one requirement among several—not the entire coating specification.

## 7. Choosing the coating by application

### High-strength bolts and screws

For property class **10.9 and 12.9** fasteners, hydrogen embrittlement becomes a major process-selection issue.

A properly specified non-electrolytic zinc-flake system to ISO 10683 or ASTM F3393 is often an excellent option because the coating deposition step does not generate hydrogen and the thin film is compatible with precision threads.

Mechanical zinc may also be suitable where permitted by the product specification and where the required coating thickness, thread fit and assembly behaviour have been validated.

For regulated structural bolting, including ASTM F3125 high-strength assemblies, do not select a coating solely from a generic coating table. The bolt standard, coating standard, assembly qualification and project specification must all permit the selected finish.

### Automotive and controlled-torque assembly

Where clamp load must be closely controlled, the coating's **coefficient of friction** becomes a design parameter.

A system such as GEOMET 500, or a zinc-flake basecoat with a qualified friction-modifying topcoat, can provide both corrosion protection and a specified friction window. Testing to **ISO 16047** is commonly used to evaluate the torque/clamp-force relationship.

This is particularly important for automated assembly because an uncontrolled change in friction can create either insufficient preload or bolt overload even when the torque wrench is correctly calibrated.

### Self-drilling screws, roofing and construction fasteners

Multilayer coatings such as RUSPERT are attractive where the coating must withstand abrasion during driving and then provide long-term atmospheric corrosion protection.

For roofing or cladding, also consider compatibility with the sheet material. Contact with aluminium, zinc-aluminium coated steel or other dissimilar metals can alter the corrosion mechanism.

Where colour matching is required, a functional topcoat can also serve an architectural purpose without changing the basic fastener geometry.

### Springs, clips, washers and heat-sensitive hardened components

Mechanical zinc can be useful when the coating process must avoid both electroplating and the high temperatures associated with hot-dip galvanizing.

However, do not assume that every zinc-flake curing cycle is harmless or harmful to every spring or heat-treated component. The acceptable thermal exposure depends on the substrate material, hardness, tempering temperature and required mechanical properties.

When spring properties or hardness are critical, the finishing process should be approved against the material's heat-treatment condition.

### Legacy Dacromet specifications

If a drawing still calls for Dacromet, first identify whether the requirement is truly brand-specific or whether it represents an older corrosion-performance specification.

A modern GEOMET or other qualified zinc-flake system may provide an environmentally preferable alternative, but substitution should be made only after reviewing:

- required corrosion performance;
- friction coefficient;
- coating thickness;
- colour;
- topcoat requirements;
- OEM or customer approvals; and
- drawing change-control requirements.

## 8. How to specify a fastener coating properly

A weak purchase description might say:

> M12 × 50, class 10.9, zinc flake.

That leaves too many engineering variables undefined.

A stronger specification would identify the coating system and its functional requirements, for example:

> M12 × 50 hex bolt, property class 10.9, zinc-flake coating to ISO 10683, Cr(VI)-free, minimum 720 h neutral salt spray to first red rust, controlled coefficient of friction 0.12–0.18 tested in accordance with ISO 16047, silver finish. Surface preparation shall be suitable for high-strength fasteners and shall minimise internal hydrogen-embrittlement risk.

Depending on the application, the purchase specification may also need to state:

- coating designation and governing standard;
- basecoat and topcoat if proprietary systems are required;
- minimum corrosion resistance;
- coefficient-of-friction range;
- coating thickness or coating class;
- colour;
- restrictions on hexavalent chromium;
- thread-gauging requirements after coating;
- hydrogen-embrittlement process controls or testing;
- adhesion requirements;
- coating compatibility between bolt, nut and washer; and
- customer or OEM qualification requirements.

## 9. A practical selection rule

For procurement work, the following logic is a useful starting point:

**If the fastener is high strength:** begin by checking hydrogen-embrittlement susceptibility and whether the governing fastener standard restricts the coating process.

**If tightening accuracy matters:** specify friction performance, not just coating type.

**If the fastener is driven aggressively:** consider coating abrasion resistance as well as corrosion resistance.

**If a thick sacrificial zinc layer is required:** mechanical zinc or hot-dip galvanizing may be more appropriate than a thin zinc-flake coating, subject to thread and material requirements.

**If the drawing specifies a proprietary coating:** do not substitute based only on salt-spray hours. Compare the complete functional specification.

## Final takeaway

There is no universally “best” zinc-based fastener coating.

**Zinc flake and GEOMET** are particularly strong choices where high-strength fasteners, thin coating build and controlled friction are important.

**Dacromet** remains relevant mainly through legacy specifications and should be reviewed carefully before substitution.

**RUSPERT** provides a useful multilayer approach for construction and driven fasteners where corrosion resistance, surface durability and functional topcoats matter.

**Mechanical zinc** provides sacrificial metallic zinc without electroplating and can be valuable where greater zinc thickness or lower process-temperature exposure is required.

The correct engineering decision comes from matching the coating to the **fastener strength, service environment, thread tolerance, tightening method and governing specification**—not from comparing salt-spray hours in isolation.
