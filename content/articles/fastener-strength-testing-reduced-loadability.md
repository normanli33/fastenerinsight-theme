# Fastener Strength Testing: Understanding 8.8 vs 08.8, A4-80 vs A4-080, and When Alternative Testing Is Required

*Why material strength alone does not guarantee the load-bearing capacity of a finished fastener.*

When purchasing high-strength fasteners, engineers and procurement professionals often rely on familiar property class designations such as **8.8, 10.9, A2-70 and A4-80**.

These markings provide important information about mechanical properties. However, they do not tell the entire story.

Consider two stainless steel screws manufactured from A4 stainless steel. One is marked **A4-80**, while the other is designated **A4-080**.

Both refer to a nominal tensile-strength level of 800 MPa, but they do not represent the same finished-product loadability.

Similarly, a carbon steel screw designated **08.8** should not automatically be considered interchangeable with a standard **8.8** bolt.

The difference lies in a fundamental engineering concept: **full loadability versus reduced loadability**.

This distinction affects product selection, manufacturing, mechanical testing, certification and ultimately the reliability of bolted connections.

## 1. Material Strength Is Not the Same as Fastener Loadability

A fastener's load-bearing capacity depends on more than the mechanical strength of its material.

Its geometry also matters.

A conventional hexagon head bolt typically has sufficient head strength, shank cross-section and thread engagement to develop the full tensile load associated with its property class.

However, certain fastener designs incorporate geometric features that can reduce their load-bearing capacity.

Examples include:

- Low-head socket screws
- Button head socket screws
- Countersunk head screws
- Screws with reduced or specially modified head geometry
- Special fasteners with reduced shank sections

In these designs, failure may occur at the head or another reduced section before the threaded portion reaches the full tensile load associated with a conventional bolt.

This is why international fastener standards distinguish between full-loadability and reduced-loadability products.

### Full-loadability fasteners

A full-loadability fastener is designed to withstand the specified minimum tensile load associated with its property class and nominal thread size, when tested under the applicable standard.

Typical examples include standard hexagon head bolts manufactured to ISO 4014 or ISO 4017.

### Reduced-loadability fasteners

A reduced-loadability fastener has geometric limitations that prevent it from achieving the full tensile load of an equivalent conventional fastener.

This does not necessarily mean that its material is weaker.

For example, a low-head socket screw may be manufactured from steel meeting the same nominal tensile-strength level as a conventional socket head cap screw, but its smaller head geometry may limit the load the finished screw can sustain.

**The critical distinction is between the strength of the material and the load-bearing capacity of the complete fastener.**

## 2. What Is the Difference Between Property Classes 8.8 and 08.8?

ISO 898-1 specifies mechanical properties for carbon steel and alloy steel bolts, screws and studs.

Under this system, property class 8.8 identifies a conventional strength classification, while the leading-zero designation 08.8 identifies a reduced-loadability classification.

| Characteristic | Class 8.8 | Class 08.8 |
|---|---|---|
| Material family | Carbon or alloy steel | Carbon or alloy steel |
| Governing mechanical-property standard | ISO 898-1 | ISO 898-1 |
| Nominal tensile-strength level | 800 MPa | 800 MPa |
| Finished-product loadability | Full | Reduced |
| Leading zero | No | Yes |
| Typical products | Standard hex bolts and socket cap screws | Certain reduced-head or special screws |
| Full tensile load based on nominal stress area | Expected where applicable | Not necessarily achievable |
| Acceptance testing | Standard full-loadability testing | Reduced-loadability provisions |

### Why does the leading zero matter?

The leading zero identifies reduced loadability.

It should not be interpreted simply as a lower material grade or a different heat treatment.

For a conventional Class 8.8 bolt, the finished product is expected to meet the relevant tensile-load requirements under ISO 898-1.

For a reduced-loadability Class 08.8 screw, the product may fail at a geometrically weaker section before reaching the full tensile load associated with Class 8.8.

Consequently, specifying 08.8 instead of 8.8 is not merely an administrative change. It can affect the suitability of the fastener for a particular application.

## 3. A4-80 vs A4-080: The Stainless Steel Equivalent

A similar distinction exists for corrosion-resistant stainless steel fasteners under ISO 3506-1.

In the designation **A4-80**:

- **A4** identifies an austenitic stainless steel grade group.
- **80** identifies a nominal minimum tensile-strength level of 800 MPa.

The designation **A4-080** incorporates a leading zero to indicate reduced loadability.

| Characteristic | A4-80 | A4-080 |
|---|---|---|
| Stainless steel group | A4 | A4 |
| Mechanical-property standard | ISO 3506-1 | ISO 3506-1 |
| Nominal minimum tensile-strength level | 800 MPa | 800 MPa |
| Loadability classification | Full | Reduced |
| Leading zero | No | Yes |
| Typical application | Standard stainless hex bolts | Certain reduced-loadability stainless screws |
| Full-size tensile load | Applicable where specified | Subject to reduced-loadability requirements |

### An important qualification

Not every short or reduced-head stainless steel screw can automatically be designated A4-080.

ISO 3506-1 includes conditions relating to product geometry, thread length and the applicability of tensile testing.

For certain reduced-loadability fasteners with insufficient threaded length, the standard restricts the use of property class designations.

Therefore, the correct designation must be determined from the applicable edition of ISO 3506-1 and the relevant product standard.

**A4-080 should never be used as a generic substitute for A4-80 without checking the fastener geometry and the applicable requirements.**

## 4. Which Fastener Products Require Special Testing Consideration?

Different head styles and dimensional configurations can affect how finished fasteners should be tested.

| Product standard | Product type | General testing consideration |
|---|---|---|
| ISO 4014 | Hexagon head bolt, partially threaded | Normally full-loadability |
| ISO 4017 | Hexagon head screw, fully threaded | Normally full-loadability |
| ISO 4762 | Hexagon socket head cap screw | Normally full-loadability |
| ISO 7380-1 | Hexagon socket button head screw | Reduced-loadability considerations |
| ISO 10642 | Hexagon socket countersunk head screw | Reduced-loadability considerations |
| DIN 7984 | Low-head hexagon socket screw | Head geometry and reduced loadability |
| Custom drawings | Special head or reduced-shank fasteners | Engineering assessment required |

The product standard, mechanical-property standard and actual dimensions must all be considered together. None of them is sufficient on its own.

## 5. When Alternative Verification Is Required

The full-size tensile test is the reference method. It requires sufficient free thread length to grip the product and apply load axially along the fastener axis.

Many reduced-loadability products cannot be gripped that way. A short countersunk screw, a button head screw or a low-head socket screw may simply not offer the free length a tensile test fixture needs — and a fastener cannot be pulled to failure through its head by a method designed to load the thread.

Where direct tensile testing is not feasible, verification moves to alternative methods:

**Proof-load testing.** A specified proof load is applied to the finished product and held, then released. The pass criterion is no permanent elongation beyond the permitted amount — the fastener is not taken to failure. This confirms the product sustains its proof load without permanent set, and is the practical substitute where full tensile testing is impossible but the geometry can still be gripped.

**Hardness testing on the finished product.** Hardness is related to tensile strength through established correlations, and the relevant standards state hardness ranges for each property class. Testing the finished part — not the raw material — is the practical way to confirm that the forming and heat-treatment route produced a product consistent with its declared class.

**Material and heat-lot certification.** Mill certificates, typically EN 10204 3.1, document the chemistry and mechanical properties of the wire or bar before forming. They are supporting evidence rather than a substitute for finished-product testing, but they are the traceable record that the input material met its specification.

Two practical rules follow from this:

1. **Agree the test method in the purchase order.** "Class 8.8" and "class 08.8" can both be quoted against the same RFQ line, and the difference only becomes visible when someone asks what the acceptance test is. Name the product standard, the property class as it should be marked, and the verification method.
2. **Do not upgrade a designation by inference.** A supplier who cannot achieve a full-loadability rating for a given geometry has not failed — the geometry does not allow it. Accepting 08.8 or A4-080 for what the drawing specifies as 8.8 or A4-80 is a change to the connection, not a paperwork convenience.

## Key takeaways

- Property class describes **both** a material strength level and a finished-product loadability classification. The second is the one that governs the connection.
- A **leading zero** (08.8, A4-080) signals reduced loadability caused by geometry — not a lower-grade material and not a different heat treatment.
- Button head, countersunk, low-head and reduced-shank designs are the usual candidates for reduced-loadability designation.
- Where geometry prevents a full-size tensile test, verification shifts to proof-load testing, finished-product hardness testing and mill certification — agreed in writing before the order is placed.
